What invisible characters cost you in LLM tokens

Short answer: about one token each. A 1,000-word text with a single zero-width space after every word goes from 1,103 tokens to 2,103 — a 90.7% increase — before you have added a single word of meaning. The characters are invisible on screen, but the tokenizer sees every one of them and you pay for every one of them.

Measured, not estimated

These are the numbers from a 1,000-word English sample, tokenized twice — once with o200k_base and once with cl100k_base, the encodings behind current OpenAI models. Only difference between rows: invisible characters inserted at a fixed interval.

Sample (1,000 words) Invisible chars Tokens (cl100k_base) Tokens (o200k_base) Increase
Clean text 0 1,103 1,103 —
One zero-width space after every word 1,000 2,103 2,065 +90.7%
One every 5 words 200 1,303 1,296 +18.1%
One every 10 words 100 1,203 1,200 +9.1%
One after every sentence 38 1,141 1,141 +3.4%

The pattern is close to linear: one invisible character in, roughly one token out. Work out the cost of any piece of text by counting the hidden characters — the token count rises by about that much.

Why a character you cannot see still costs a token

Tokenizers are built from byte-pair merges learned on ordinary text. Common English words collapse into single tokens. Zero-width and invisible characters are rare in training data, so they never earned a merge of their own — the tokenizer falls back to splitting them into their raw UTF-8 bytes, and each of those bytes becomes a token. U+200B is three bytes in UTF-8 (E2 80 8B), which is why one invisible space lands you roughly one extra token rather than zero.

That is the whole mechanism. There is nothing exotic about it, which is exactly why it gets missed: everything about these characters says they are not there.

Where this actually bites

  • Pasting model output back into a model. The most common way to carry a watermark forward is to feed yesterday's output into today's prompt. You pay for the hidden characters on every round trip.
  • RAG pipelines. Chunks scraped from pages or documents that carry invisible characters inflate every retrieval, and the inflation is invisible in your usage dashboard because the text looks short.
  • Batch jobs. A 90% token increase on a nightly job over a large corpus is not a rounding error, and it does not show up as an error at all.
  • Context limits. Tokens spent on characters that carry no meaning are tokens unavailable for the content you actually wanted in the window.

What it costs to hide an instruction

A watermark only has to be present. A hidden instruction has to be read. Both are carried the same way, so the same question applies: what does it cost to put a sentence into a document where nobody can see it? Measured, hiding one 72-character sentence costs about 24 times what typing it in the open would — roughly 280 tokens instead of 12. And the carriers that cost the least to hide are the ones that survive the most cleaning.

Carrier Code points UTF-8 bytes Tokens (o200k_base) Tokens (cl100k_base) Compared with typing it out
Zero-width binary: U+200B for 0, U+200C for 1, eight per byte 576 1,728 347 471 28.9×
Tag characters: U+E0000 plus the character code, one per character 72 288 277 216 23.1×
Variation selectors: U+E0100 plus the character code, one per character 72 288 288 287 24.0×
The same sentence, typed in the open 72 72 12 12 1×

Put into a 1,000-word document that costs 1,140 tokens on its own, that payload adds 24.4% to 30.4% to the bill. The same sentence typed in the open adds 1.05%. Hiding text is not the cheap version of sending it. Per character it is between 23 and 29 times more expensive, and the cheapest of the three is not the one most people watch for. Measured one at a time inside a word: a variation selector costs five tokens in both encodings, a tag character five in o200k_base and four in cl100k_base, a zero-width space two.

The more awkward part is what survives. Seven ordinary cleaning steps were run against all three payloads, in JavaScript and in Python, and the two languages agreed on every row.

Cleaning step Zero-width binary Tag characters Variation selectors
trim() / strip() intact intact intact
NFC, NFD, NFKC, NFKD intact intact intact
The popular [\u200B-\u200D\uFEFF] gone intact intact
\p{C}, everything in the Other category gone gone intact
\p{Cf}, format characters only gone gone intact
\p{M}, all combining marks intact intact gone
ASCII-only filter gone gone gone

The name people reach for when they want to strip invisible characters is \p{C}, and it works on two of the three. It leaves the variation-selector payload completely readable: all 72 characters came back out of the cleaned string. The reason is a classification detail rather than a clever trick — U+E0100 to U+E01EF are Mn, combining marks, not C. A selector with nothing to select still takes up a code point, still carries its bytes, and still costs its tokens. In Chromium at 16 px it measured 0.00 px wide with zero painted pixels, against 503.06 px and 2,619 painted pixels for the same sentence typed in the open.

Which sounds like an argument for stripping all combining marks instead, and that is where the trade-off actually sits. Run \p{M} over a single line of ordinary text — three accented words, two emoji that carry a presentation selector, and two words in Devanagari — and it removed 9 of 41 code points in decomposed form, 5 of 37 in composed form. Every accent went, both emoji lost the selector that makes them render as emoji rather than as the plain black symbol, and the Devanagari words lost three of the signs they need, which leaves them misspelled rather than merely unaccented. The choice is not between cleaning and not cleaning. It is between which kind of real text you are willing to damage, and there is no setting that damages only the part you wanted gone.

Detection has the same shape. Blocked together at the end of a document, the three payloads produce runs of 576, 72 and 72 consecutive invisible code points and are caught immediately by any scanner that flags three or more in a row. Spread one code point per word, the longest run of invisible characters in the whole document is one, and such a scanner sees nothing at all. Spreading costs nothing: with the payload spread, the rendered box measured 1280 × 201.52 px, exactly what the clean version of the same paragraph measured, and the token count was unchanged. The one-code-point carriers need 7.2% of the words to carry one; the binary carrier needs 57.6%.

Nothing in the ordinary path removes it either. After selecting the whole rendered paragraph and pressing Ctrl+C, the full 72-character sentence was recovered intact from the clipboard, with the payload spread one code point per word. innerText and textContent both returned 1,585 characters — the page's own view of itself does not drop any of it. Whether a model then obeys a hidden instruction is a separate question from whether it receives one, and it is the question people argue about. The receiving end is not in dispute: the characters are in the token stream, they are billed, and no step in a normal copy-and-paste takes them out. Of the seven cleaning steps above, only the ASCII-only filter removed all three.

Measured 2026-10-02 with tiktoken on a 1,000-word English sample, in both o200k_base and cl100k_base; survival checked in Python against Unicode 15.1 and in Node against Unicode 17, which returned the same answer for every row; rendering and clipboard measured in Chromium.

Check your own text

Count the invisible characters first, then multiply. The zero-width space remover shows you where they sit and how many there are; the invisible characters list has the code points if you want to look them up by hand. If you are cleaning a payload rather than prose, the JSON cleaner and Excel cell cleaner do the same job on structured input.

Reproduce it

The measurement above is three lines of Python. Run it on your own text rather than trusting a table on someone else's site:

import tiktoken
enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("o200k_base")   # or "cl100k_base"
dirty = "Your text here"                    # paste as-is, do not clean it
clean  = dirty.replace("\u200b", "").replace("\u200c", "") \
                .replace("\u200d", "").replace("\ufeff", "")
print(len(enc.encode(dirty)), len(enc.encode(clean)),
      len(enc.encode(dirty)) - len(enc.encode(clean)))

The third number is what the hidden characters are costing you per call. Multiply it by your request volume and your provider's rate.

What this does not tell you

This page measures cost, not authorship. Extra tokens tell you characters are present; they do not tell you who put them there or why, and they are not evidence that a text was generated by a model. Token counts also vary by encoding, so the percentages above apply to the two encodings tested — run the snippet against your own model's tokenizer if you need a number you can bill against.
