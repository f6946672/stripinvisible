Remove text from image: what the patch leaves behind is the whole question

Short answer: by the time you have an image there are no characters left to remove — there are pixels, and the pixels the text occupied have to be replaced with something that passes for the background that was behind it. So the question is not whether the text is gone, it is whether the patch shows. Measured in Chromium 153, that is one number: the deepest difference the patch still has against the background around it. Over a 228 × 46 box on a background that varies by 5, filling it with one sampled colour leaves 5, blurring it in place leaves 130 and interpolating across it leaves 2. The threshold that decides whether that matters is not fixed either — it is the background's own variation, and the same measurement in the other direction says whether text is in the picture at all: a glyph is still fully recoverable at a contrast of 1/255, the smallest an 8-bit image can hold, and it needs one more than twice the background's variation before the whole of it clears.

The samples carry a copy of their own background without the text on it, so those readings are the numbers quoted below; a picture you load has no such copy, so there the reading is taken against a straight line fitted across the box, and the tool reports the two readings and the share between them rather than a verdict — the background in a loaded picture cannot be measured cleanly enough to set one.

    One number, both directions

    Text in an image and the removal of text from an image are the same question asked twice, and both times it is asked in pixels. To know whether there is text in a region you ask how far the pixels in that region sit from the background. To know whether a removal worked you ask how far the pixels that replaced them sit from the background. It is one measurement taken before and after, and the only thing that changes is which side of the threshold you want to be on: above it, and the text is there; below it, and the patch is invisible.

    That is why the two halves of this page belong together, and why a number is worth having at all. The word the tooling around this uses for the second half is seamless, and the eight tool pages that rank for this term carry no measurement of it between them — not in their titles and not in their descriptions. So the rest of this page is the number, in both directions, and what moves it.

    Text is still fully there at 1/255

    Start with the direction that surprises people. An 8-bit image cannot express a difference smaller than 1, so 1 is the floor: text drawn one step away from the background is as invisible as text in an image can be. It is not, however, absent. Measured in Chromium 153 on a 480 × 220 canvas, with Sample text at 600 40px Arial on a flat background of 240 and a 228 × 46 box around it, the glyph occupies 2,745 pixels at full contrast, and it occupies the same 2,745 pixels at a difference of 1. Scaling the difference back up by 255 recovers 100 per cent of them, at every contrast on the way down.

    The row worth stopping on is the first. There is no contrast at which the glyph thins out or loses its faintest edges, because the anti-aliasing that softens an edge is itself a difference, and at a difference of 1 the softness survives as a difference of 1. What the floor costs you is not information, it is the ability to notice: nothing on a screen shows a step of 1 as anything other than the same colour. So a picture can carry text that no one looking at it can see, and the file holds all of it.

    The threshold is twice the background's own variation

    Which raises the obvious follow-up: if 1 is enough, why is text ever unrecoverable? Because a real background is not one value. It is a field that varies, and a glyph only stands out from it while it differs from it by more than the field differs from itself. Measured over six noise amplitudes, with the same 2,745-pixel glyph, the smallest contrast at which every pixel of the glyph clears the background is one more than twice the background's own maximum deviation:

    The factor of two is not slack in the measurement, and it is worth spelling out because it is the part that decides whether text survives in a picture. A glyph pixel is not painted onto a blank sheet: it replaces a background pixel, so what a reader can recover from it is the contrast plus whatever the background was already doing there. The glyph has to clear the background's own spread, and the worst pixel in the glyph is the one sitting on the lowest part of that spread, where the background is working against the contrast rather than with it. Measured at exactly twice the spread, 649 of the 2,745 pixels are still inside the background; one step higher and all of them are out.

    The practical reading is that the same invisible text is plainly written on a flat fill and genuinely gone on a photograph, and that nothing about the text changed — only the picture it was written on. It also means the useful question is never “how faint can text be” but “how faint can it be and still beat this particular background”, which is a question about the picture rather than about the text.

    What each way of removing it leaves behind

    Now the other direction, and it is the same number. The three ordinary ways of clearing a region are to fill it with one colour sampled from nearby, to blur it in place, and to interpolate across it from the edges. Each was run over the same box of text on three backgrounds, and the result is the deepest difference between the patched region and the background that was actually behind the text — that is, what a viewer would see as the patch:

    Three things fall out of that table. The first is that blurring is not a removal at all: at 126 to 131 it leaves a difference larger than the text it was covering, so it replaces something you could read with something you can see. The second is that interpolating beats a flat fill on a background that varies in one direction and loses to it on one that varies in every direction — 2 against 5 on the gradient, 12 against 6 on the noise — because a straight line drawn across a noisy region is a smooth band, and a smooth band is exactly what noise is not. The third is that the flat fill lands on the background's own variation and stops there: 5 where the background varies by 5, 6 where it varies by 6, and 0 where it does not vary at all.

    The connection to the previous section is what decides whether any of this shows. A patch of 6 is invisible on a background that varies by 16 and obvious on one that varies by 2, because visibility is a comparison rather than a value. That is why the same tool produces a clean result on one picture and a smear on the next, and why “seamless” is not a property a tool has. It is a property of a tool against a particular background, and the only way to know which you have is to measure the picture you are actually working on — which is what the box above does.

    AI watermark remover: a mark is mixed into the picture, not painted over it

    The word in front of the tool is doing two different jobs, and only one of them needs a model. A watermark is not a patch of different pixels sitting on top of a picture; it is the picture, scaled down and mixed with one colour. Every pixel under it holds (1 − a) of the background plus a of the mark, where a is how strong the mark is at that pixel. At a = 1 the background is gone and the only way forward is to invent pixels. At anything below that the background is still in the file, and getting it back is arithmetic rather than imagination: background = (pixel − a × mark colour) ÷ (1 − a).

    Measured in Chromium 153 over the same 228 × 46 box the rest of this page uses, on the flat sample with a background of 240 and a mark colour of 30. Two things in that table are worth stopping on. The first is the row at 0.75: the blend lands on 82.5, an 8-bit pixel has to write 82, and dividing by (1 − a) turns that half-step into a whole 2 — the same rounding at a strength of 0.5 costs nothing, because there the blend lands on a whole number. The second is the last column: being wrong about the strength by one hundredth leaves 1 of 255 at 0.1 and 15 at 0.9, because what the mistake is multiplied by is the gap between the background and the mark colour, divided by how little of the background is left. The arithmetic is exact and unforgiving at the same time.

    It is also per pixel, and that is what decides whether it can be used at all. An overlay whose strength runs from 0.2 on one side to 0.8 on the other — which is what the soft edge of a logo is — undone with one number, its own average of 0.5, leaves 126 of 255. Undone with the strength of each column, the same formula leaves 5. What has to be known is not a strength but a shape: where the mark is, and how strong it is at every pixel.

    A strength can be read off the picture, though, where the mark covers a patch with texture in it, because blending scales the variation down along with the values: the spread of the pixels under the mark is (1 − a) times the spread of the same patch without it. Measured on the gradient sample that recovers 0.2526 for a true 0.25, 0.496 for 0.5, 0.7379 for 0.75 and 0.8987 for 0.9. Run it end to end — read the strength, then undo the blend with it — and it leaves 1, 3, 12 and 9 on that background, and 2, 3, 19 and 35 on the noisy one.

    Set those against the three ways of clearing the same box and the picture changes. On the noisy background, filling the box with one colour sampled from around it leaves 6, and interpolating between its edges leaves 12, whatever the strength of the mark was. So at 0.25 and 0.5 the arithmetic wins comfortably, and at 0.9 — fed the strength it measured for itself — it leaves 35, nearly six times what the crudest guess leaves. Being able to divide the mark back out is worth a lot while the mark is weak, and it is worth nothing once being slightly wrong about it costs more than guessing.

    That is the line between the two jobs, and it is also the answer to the two things people say a model is for here. One is that it imagines the picture without the mark in it; the other is that it is a filter being run over an image that has noise in it. Both are right about a different half. Where the mark is translucent and its shape is known, there is nothing to imagine and nothing to filter: the background is in the file and a division takes it back out. Where the mark is opaque, or its shape is not known, (1 − a) is 0 and every pixel in the region has to be invented — which is exactly the case the rest of this page measures, because text sits at a = 1. And whatever invents them is still held to the same threshold as everything else here: what it puts back has to differ from the background by less than the background differs from itself.

    One last thing the numbers settle. A faint mark is not a nearly absent mark. At a strength of 0.1 it still moves every pixel under it by 22 of 255, which is nowhere near the floor this page measured for text, and all of it comes back when the blend is undone. What makes a watermark hard to deal with is never that it is faint; it is that its shape is unknown, and that one wrong number about it is multiplied by everything the blend took away.

    What this page does not tell you

    How any particular tool works. Every number here was produced by this browser drawing into a canvas and reading the pixels back, and no service was queried. A tool that reconstructs a region from a learned model is not doing any of the three operations above and can beat all three of them; what it cannot do is change what counts as clean, because that is set by the picture it was handed.

    The measurements are one string at one size and weight in one font stack, and the 2,745 pixels are a property of that string at that size, not a constant. The noisy background is uniform noise at a known amplitude rather than a photograph, which is structured; structure is what a reconstruction can exploit and uniform noise is the case where it has nothing to work with, so a photograph will generally land between the gradient column and the noisy one. The comparison between the three methods is the point of the table, and the exact figures will move with the text, the box and the background.
