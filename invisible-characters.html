Invisible Unicode Characters: The Complete List

One click puts the character on your clipboard. Nothing to select, nothing to download — press the button, then paste it where you need it.

Want them out of your text instead of reading about them? Use the zero-width space remover, the Claude watermark remover, the JSON cleaner or the Excel cell cleaner or the Word and Docs cleaner. Some of them can carry text: see Unicode tag characters.

These characters take up no room, print nothing, and survive copy and paste. Most of them exist for a good reason — telling a browser where a word may break, holding an emoji family together, setting text direction for Arabic. The trouble starts when they travel somewhere they were not meant to be: into a database key, a JSON string, a search box, or text copied out of a chat window.

Every character below is listed with what it actually does and whether deleting it is safe. The copy button puts the character itself on your clipboard, which is handy when you need one to test with. Everything on this page is standard invisible Unicode — no font trick, no image, nothing to install.

What you just copied

If you searched for invisible character copy and paste, the button at the top is the whole answer: U+200B has no width, prints nothing, and survives the trip through the clipboard into almost any input box. It is one codepoint, not a trick.

The same codepoint is what most people mean by invisible text copy: a string built out of characters that render as nothing. A field holding only those is empty to look at and not empty to the software reading it, which is why some forms accept it and others hand it back. Nothing about the character predicts which — paste it and see.

Where invisible text copy and paste usually goes wrong is length, not the character. A single one tucked inside a word is invisible and harmless. A long run of them in a field that is supposed to be blank reads as noise to anything checking that field, because to it the field is not empty — it is full of something it cannot display.

There is no invisible font

Searches for an invisible font start from a sensible guess: somewhere out there is a typeface that draws nothing. Fonts do not work that way. A font maps each character to a glyph, and a character whose glyph happens to be blank is still a character — it still counts toward the length of your string, it still gets copied, and it still shows up in a diff. What those generators actually do is swap in invisible characters, not switch typeface.

That distinction is worth keeping, because a generator hands you characters you did not pick and cannot see, and some of those carry meaning. The joiner at U+200D is what holds an emoji family together as one picture; deleting it changes what people see. Copying from the table here means you know exactly which codepoint you ended up with.

For a blank name or a blank bio

A blank space copy is not the space bar. Most forms trim U+0020 off both ends of a name, which is why a name built out of ordinary spaces comes back as empty. U+00A0 is a different codepoint that looks identical and is not trimmed the same way. That is the one to reach for when a form refuses a name made of spaces.

People after an invisible blank usually have the same problem from the other side: the field has to look like something is in it, even though nothing should show. Either character does that job. Which one a given site accepts is decided by that site, not by Unicode, so if one is rejected it is worth trying the other before concluding the field cannot be blanked at all.

Why a blank name sometimes gets rejected

The search blank space copy and paste instagram is the job above with one extra constraint: the check runs on their side, and it changes without notice. A character that went through last month can start coming back as invalid. Copy one, paste it, save, then look at the profile from a second device before assuming it held.

What gets called an invisible name symbol is the same handful of codepoints under a different name. When U+200B is refused, the usual fallback is U+3164, the Hangul filler — a real letter slot that renders blank in most fonts rather than a zero-width mark, so it takes up room instead of collapsing to nothing. That difference is also why it behaves differently in fields that measure width.

Why they end up in ordinary text

Three sources cover almost all of it. Rich text editors insert zero width spaces and no-break spaces as you type, and they come along when you copy. AI chat output carries invisible marks that identify it. And text copied from a web page picks up whatever the page was using for layout.

None of those are visible, which is the whole problem. A string that renders identically to another one can still fail to match it.

A note on the other side of this

The same characters are also used to hide text in a URL, to smuggle words past a filter, and to fingerprint text so its origin can be traced later. That is worth knowing, because it explains why some of them show up in places they were never typed. This page exists to help you find them and take them out, not to help you hide anything.
